# Choosing findings and emphasis

## Decide what deserves the reader's attention

Before drafting results or a summary, identify findings that do at least one of these jobs:

- Answer an explicit research question.
- Establish whether a central comparison is credible.
- Distinguish competing explanations or design choices.
- Reveal a failure, trade-off, unexpected behaviour, or boundary that changes the answer.
- Support a useful and proportionate implication.

For each candidate, record the observed quantity, comparison, conditions, evidence location, and strongest supportable claim. Check credibility before deciding how prominently to feature it. A large improvement from an unmatched comparison is not a strong headline. When credible findings support different emphases, recommend a priority with a reason and ask which contribution the user intends to foreground. Keep material conflicting evidence visible regardless of that preference.

## Allocate space by importance

| Role | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Central finding | Explain early, provide the main supporting evidence, and interpret its importance |
| Necessary credibility check | Keep enough in the main report for the reader to judge the central claim |
| Supporting detail | Summarise and point to a table, figure, or appendix |
| Material conflicting or negative evidence | Include near the claim it qualifies |
| Peripheral diagnostic detail | Retain in supporting material if useful; avoid interrupting the main argument |

Use judgement, not an artificial scoring formula. Respect any pre-specified outcomes or required reporting order. Do not hide unfavourable findings, excluded cases, or essential validation evidence in an appendix merely to improve the story. Distinguish selective explanation from selective reporting.

## Highlight by making meaning explicit

State the finding near the beginning of its subsection or paragraph. Give a meaningful comparison and enough evidence to evaluate it. Then explain what it establishes for the question being asked. Use emphasis in wording and order; typography is a separate task.

Replace evaluations such as "the most interesting result" with the reason the result matters. Where the evidence is weak, make the uncertainty itself part of the finding.

Illustrative example, not a project result: a setting reduces a numerical error measure while a key physical inventory continues to drift. The report may need to emphasise the remaining limit on interpretation alongside the numerical improvement. It should not describe overall physical accuracy as improved on the basis of that error measure alone.

Basis: [MIT Communication Lab, Results](https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/be/commkit/journal-article-results/). The selection questions and treatment table are original operational guidance.
