# Principles: think in relationships

This is an operational synthesis, not a fixed aesthetic or a validated model of
human perception. Source notes are in [sources](sources.md). Apply a principle
only when its mechanism helps the brief; judge the result in the render.

## Three orders, one composition

**Depth** describes apparent front/back relationships. **Attention** describes
visual prominence. **Reading** describes the intended information sequence.
Maintain them separately: a legend may overlay an image but be discovered late;
a title may be read first without being the largest visual mass. This separation
extends the hierarchy and elevation ideas in [S5](sources.md#s5) and
[S6](sources.md#s6); it is this skill's working model.

A stack of objects with different z-values is not automatically perceptually
layered. Name the visible evidence for each important relationship.

## Figure-ground and local contrast

Treat the space around an important object as design material. Getty distinguishes
positive and negative space; NN/G explains hierarchy through relative visual
properties ([S7](sources.md#s7), [S8](sources.md#s8)).

**Apply:** inspect the main subject's silhouette, not only its centre. A busy edge
behind a vessel can erase the vessel's outline. Move the crop, simplify that local
background region, or provide a calm reading area. Preserve expressive detail
elsewhere when the brief calls for energy.

**Check:** can a viewer recognise the subject at a small size? Does text retain
stable contrast across its entire background, not just at one sampled pixel?
Uniformly fading the whole background is one option, not the default repair.

## Attention is a limited resource

Identify the anchor and its strongest competitor. Prefer a clear primary emphasis
and subordinate groups; use a balanced comparison when the task requires equal
attention. Concentrated emphasis is informed by [S1](sources.md#s1).

**Apply:** distribute scale, contrast, saturation, edge detail, and isolation
selectively. Often a quieter container is enough to let its content belong to a
supporting layer. Preserve the content's readability while reducing decoration.

**Check:** does making every section larger or brighter merely move the conflict?
There is no universal numeric attention score or required foreground percentage.

## Depth cues must agree

[S6](sources.md#s6) supplies a surface/elevation model; the choices below adapt
it for mixed editorial and scientific compositions rather than copying UI values.

| Cue | Useful application | Failure to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Occlusion | One outline continues behind another, establishing local front/back order. | Covering data, labels, essential geometry, or the very boundary being explained. |
| Scale and crop | Let the main object cross a frame or use a crop that implies a larger environment. | Distorting scientific geometry or mistaking a large area for guaranteed attention. |
| Edge and detail | Keep the anchor identifiable; use broader, quieter detail in atmospheric regions. | Blurring evidence or making secondary text unreadable. |
| Value and colour | Create separation against the immediate neighbour. | Treating warm/cool, dark/light, or saturated/muted as universal depth laws. |
| Light and shadow | Give an illustrated object a consistent light direction or a justified contact/elevation cue. | Applying the same shadow to everything, so nothing occupies a distinct plane. |
| Transparency | Reveal a genuine relationship between a shell, internal feature, and background. | Mixing layers until neither silhouette nor text can be read. |

Use the few cues that resolve the relationship. Flat typography and shapes can
still produce a strong composition. Literal perspective is useful only when it
clarifies the subject; text and charts need not tilt with an illustration.

## Middle ground: organise understanding

The middle ground is not leftover space. Give it the job of connecting the anchor
to supporting information. Proximity supports perceived grouping
([S9](sources.md#s9)).

**Apply:** place a figure, caption, and related callout together; separate that
cluster from unrelated content. Use alignment, repeated spacing, shared edges,
and restrained surfaces before adding another box. Make a deliberate gap or
clear overlap where edges meet; avoid accidental near-touches.

**Check:** can the grouping be understood without following a maze of connectors?
Leader lines must identify actual targets. Decorative arrows must not masquerade
as scientific flow direction or an experimental sequence.

## Typography and small details have jobs

A title identifies. A subtitle frames. Captions explain. Fine labels locate.
Treat these as roles rather than a ladder of increasingly decorative styles.
An optional eyebrow, short rule, or small label earns its place by adding context,
aiding alignment, or connecting elements. Remove it when it merely fills a gap.

**Apply:** keep readable text on protected surfaces; use spacing and weight to
support its role. Let the illustration break a frame without forcing paragraphs
into awkward narrow shapes around it. Preserve required logos and attribution.

**Check:** do details help at close range while staying quiet at a glance?
Visual-only work may propose copy changes separately, but must not quietly rewrite
scientific captions or introduce a more impressive claim.

## Rhythm and negative space

Alternate dense information with calm regions. Make the main masses work before
polishing small effects. Grayscale inspection is a useful diagnostic informed by
[S10](sources.md#s10), not a ban on expressive colour.

**Apply:** inspect a reduced or lightly blurred preview to see competing masses,
then return to full detail for reading checks. Keep diagnostics separate from the
source artwork. Content intended for comparison may need repeated aligned panels,
not a single dramatic hero. Choose the spatial model for the task.
