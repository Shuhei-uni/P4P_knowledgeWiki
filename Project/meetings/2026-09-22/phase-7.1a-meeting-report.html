The story in one minute

The historical V1 absorber was correctly implemented, but it was effectively starved of liquid: inventory grew, continuity did not settle, and little liquid reached the lower absorption zone.

The Server-3 bottom-pressure-outlet diagnostic shows a potentially useful liquid-routing mechanism, but it also allows vapour to escape. It is incomplete and needs an artificial bottom pressure-control treatment before it can be assessed as a liquid-removal route.

V2 is more promising: its new virtual outlet demonstrably tracks the inlet-derived liquid-removal command once liquid arrives in the lower zone. It has not yet produced bounded inventory, source-inclusive closure, or steady convergence.

1. V1 baseline — why the original absorber did not solve the problem

V1 completed its 500-iteration discovery horizon and remains a useful diagnostic reference. It did not approach a usable steady state: total liquid inventory rose by 284.63 kg over native iterations 1,250–1,500, while continuity stayed of order one.

The key result is not a missing source. The integrated phase-2 absorber source was −116.92 kg/s, matching its command. The lower-zone liquid-mass report nevertheless stayed zero while liquid accumulated elsewhere.

What V1 isolates

  1. Source realization: the phase-2 absorber was present, selective, and auditable.
  2. Field response: the developed field did not deliver useful liquid to that lower region.
  3. Numerical state: reverse flow and turbulent-viscosity limiting occurred at every solved iteration; no steady-state claim follows.

2. Bottom pressure outlet — useful routing signal, but vapour leakage is a design constraint

The independent Server-3 C8 family first developed an all-wall field for 5,000 active iterations. After a predeclared lower-liquid trigger, only the thin outer bottom ring was changed to a 1.120 MPa gauge pressure outlet; the remaining four bottom bands stayed walls.

Observed partial state

At native iteration 7,800 (C8-P0 active 2,800), the ring reports about −1,720.11 kg/s phase 2 and −8.32 kg/s phase 1. The stored signs are not independently normalized in this package; the defensible observation is that the ring is strongly liquid-dominated but not phase-exclusive.

What this means

The test supplies a plausible artificial liquid-routing mechanism. It does not yet demonstrate acceptable vapour loss, mass closure, stable inventory, or a selected outlet pressure. A controlled artificial bottom pressure treatment is needed to constrain steam escape.

3. V2 inlet-loading ramp — the virtual outlet begins to work once liquid arrives

V2 changes the removal law rather than simply increasing source strength. Its 715-cell lower virtual-outlet zone removes phase 2 in proportion to locally available phase-2 volume fraction; its command follows the instantaneous liquid-inlet throughput. It does not directly delete phase 1.

The initial lower region was effectively vapour-only, so realized removal started near zero as expected. During the inlet ramp, liquid developed into the lower zone and the source became active. From iteration 501 onward, the named removal and native applied phase-2 source differed by at most approximately 3e-13 kg/s.

Meeting conclusion and next decision

Phase 7.1A has moved from a correctly applied but inaccessible V1 absorber to a V2 outlet that demonstrably realizes its inlet-following phase-2 command after liquid develops in the lower zone. That is a meaningful implementation and liquid-development improvement.

The system is not yet solved: V2 inventory still grows, continuity worsens between iterations 2,000 and 4,002, pressure-outlet reverse flow persists, and phase-2 carryover at the steam outlet increases. C8 adds a useful liquid-routing signal but also exposes vapour loss through the artificial bottom boundary.

The next run should decide from all of the following together:

  • source-inclusive, phase-resolved mass closure including storage;
  • a late-window liquid-inventory slope;
  • controlled vapour flux through an artificial bottom pressure-control boundary;
  • credible phase routing; and
  • finite, improving residual behaviour.
