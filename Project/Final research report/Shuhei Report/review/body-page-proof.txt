1. Introduction
1.1. Engineering problem
Geothermal steam–water separators supply dry steam to turbines. Liquid carryover can cause
erosion and transport minerals that form deposits. Design must therefore consider separation
efficiency, pressure drop and operating pressure. (Zarrouk & Purnanto, 2015)
This study develops an inherited computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model of a vertical
bottom-outlet cyclone with a spiral inlet. It follows liquid from inlet placement through wall
transport and collection.
1.2. Problem in the inherited model
The simplified geometry omits the lower brine-discharge path. A closed bottom gives liquid no
intended exit; opening the whole lower boundary can also let steam escape there. The model
needs a collection treatment that preserves the steam route.
A virtual collector, termed an absorber, and reduced-feed startup enabled roughness and Eulerian
Wall Film (EWF) studies after unsuccessful full-geometry drainage trials.
A developed flow field is a saved full-feed state used as a common starting point, or parent. The
selected parent retained a substantial liquid-balance error; it supported comparisons but was not
validated.
1.3. Research aim and questions
The aim is to develop and assess the separator model’s liquid-transport and collection paths.
Predicting separation efficiency and pressure drop is the longer-term purpose. The present work
addresses four questions:
   1. How do mesh representation and inlet phase placement affect liquid distribution and re-
      tention near the wall?
   2. How can numerical collection and staged startup provide starting fields for wall studies?
   3. How do wall roughness and wall-film modelling affect liquid transport, storage, release
      and collection?
   4. What numerical and liquid-accounting evidence supports these predictions, and what re-
      mains necessary for field validation?
1.4. Scope and contribution
The study mainly uses the simplified primary separator. Mesh and inlet comparisons lead into
collector development, startup and wall-treatment studies. Later cases examine film develop-
ment to 500 ms, release mechanisms and direct drainage. Downstream equipment, mineral
chemistry and optimum operating pressure are outside the simulated scope.
The contribution is an account of predicted liquid destinations and methods that enable their
study. A final comparison across five mesh resolutions is reserved for Phase 9; mesh conver-
gence remains unqualified. Controlled reconstruction cases are defined in Appendix A.2.
Section 3 describes the model and comparisons. Section 4 presents the results; Sections 5–6
discuss their meaning, limits and further work.

2. Literature review
2.1. How a geothermal steam–water separator works
A vertical bottom-outlet cyclone (BOC) uses a tangential or spiral inlet to rotate the incoming
steam and brine. Liquid moves towards the wall while steam occupies the central region. An
internal pipe carries steam out through the bottom; brine leaves separately. Bottom outlet refers
to the steam-pipe arrangement. (Zarrouk & Purnanto, 2015)
Wall capture is only part of separation. A film may drain, remain in the vessel or release droplets
into the steam (Figure 1). The model must therefore distinguish wall retention from external
collection.

Uncertain inlet sizes and continued droplet generation complicate prediction. (Pointon et al.,
2009)
Higher velocity can strengthen outward transport but also increase entrainment by steam shear.
Empirical data show rapid wetness increases at different velocities for different separators (Fig-

2.3. Established design methods and geothermal CFD
Empirical geothermal design methods provide reference points for CFD. However, CFD’s addi-
tional flow detail must be assessed against its assumptions and comparison evidence. (Zarrouk
& Purnanto, 2015)
Pointon et al. (2009) used Fluent’s Discrete Phase Model (DPM) with assumed droplet feed and
wall/baffle collection. Predicted efficiencies were 99.96% for scrolled and 99.93% for tangential
entry; an empirical design method gave 99.955%. Scrolled entry also gave higher tangential
velocities (Figure 3). Empirical agreement did not independently test the feed or collection
assumptions. (Pointon et al., 2009)

DPM outlet-quality calculation. Changed vessel proportions prevented isolation of inlet shape.
Counting incomplete droplet tracks as separated improved empirical agreement, but the authors
called that assumption non-rigorous. The inherited modelling basis therefore requires explicit
treatment of unresolved droplet fate. (Purnanto et al., 2013)

2.4. Wall–liquid transport and carryover
Film motion depends on gravity, pressure and shear. Deposition adds liquid; drainage removes
it; entrainment returns droplets to the steam. More wall deposition can therefore increase storage
or later release without increasing external collection.
Rizaldy et al. (2016) predict entrainment sensitivity to loading, film thickness and velocity. Fig-
ure 4 shows conceptual release mechanisms. Missing brine-flow data prevented the required
comparison with Wairakei drain-pot measurements, leaving quantitative field agreement unre-
solved. (Rizaldy et al., 2016)
Fluent roughness changes near-wall momentum transfer; it does not add a film store or drain.
EWF instead adds surface storage and transport. Their outlet-flow responses must be read along-
side retained mass and collection. (Ansys, Inc., 2025k)
2.5. Representing liquid and its collection
The Mixture model represents bulk liquid through secondary-phase volume fraction. Algebraic
slip estimates relative phase velocity using local equilibrium. Liquid volume fraction does not
directly resolve film thickness or droplet-size distribution. (Ansys, Inc., 2025d)
DPM follows size-dependent droplet trajectories. Its interpretation requires defined injection
and boundary treatments, with every tracked fate counted, including incomplete trajectories.

The inherited model’s incomplete liquid path links the research questions: mesh and inlet place-

and release. Liquid accounting and numerical checks define the limits of each finding.

3. Model and methods
3.1. Computational domain and modelling scope
The model represented the upper separation region of a vertical cyclone: spiral inlet, vessel
walls and steam outlet. It omitted the brine pool and discharge. The bottom remained a wall, so
uncollected liquid could accumulate or leave through the steam outlet.
A numerical collector represented transfer into the omitted pool without opening a steam exit at
the base. Full-geometry trials had not established stable drainage and pool control (Appendix
A.13). Pool motion and downstream discharge remained outside the model. Absorber-free cases
disabled collection in the same domain.
The mesh uses vertical 𝑦, with the bottom near 𝑦 = 0 (exact bounds in Appendix A.1). The
steam-outlet label marks the computational boundary at the central pipe opening. Bulk flow used
a steady formulation, while film evolved on a separate clock. Bulk startup described numerical
development through iterations, not physical startup time.
3.2. Physical model and assumptions

differential-viscosity and swirl options with standard wall functions. Gravity acted in negative 𝑦
at 9.81 m/s². Energy was disabled, excluding flashing, condensation and temperature-dependent

3.2.2. Wall roughness and wall-film representation
Roughness changed wall friction through equivalent sand-grain height 𝑘𝑠 and constant 𝐶𝑠 .
Tested values were sensitivity inputs, not measured surface properties. The wall treatment and
unresolved settings are documented in Appendix A.10. (Ansys, Inc., 2025k)
Eulerian Wall Film (EWF) solved film mass and momentum on selected walls. Phase accretion
transferred bulk liquid into the film. Early cases, including development to 500 ms, included
gravity, gas shear, wall-viscous resistance and momentum advection. Film pressure-gradient,
spreading and surface-tension forces were off, as were droplet collection, splashing, stripping
and droplet edge separation. Native film-edge outflow remained active. (Ansys, Inc., 2025e)
Later tests enabled those forces and droplet mechanisms. Stripping releases film liquid under
gas forcing; splashing creates secondary droplets on impact; edge separation releases liquid at
wall edges. These tests used different developed bulk fields, so their comparison with the early
run did not isolate one mechanism. Film-to-bulk momentum feedback stayed off. Coupling
the film equations was a separate numerical choice (Appendices A.9 and A.12). (Ansys, Inc.,
2025b)

3.5. Numerical solution procedure
Original startup used Hybrid Initialisation, smooth walls, EWF off and throughput-controlled
collection. A failed command left 25% feed to iteration 1580. The corrected procedure increased
both phase commands over 2000 updates. At full feed, SIMPLE changed to Coupled with Global
Time Step. After warm-up, a separate 1000-update control supplied the iteration-5586 parent
(Appendix A.5).
The developed parent was selected after reviewing residuals, inventories, flows and sources,
without claiming convergence. Shortened startup reused iteration-1580 fields without reinitial-
isation and enabled contact collection, 0.5 mm roughness, Coupled flow and dry film together
(Appendix A.16).
Coupled solved pressure and momentum corrections together, with Mixture volume fraction
solved separately. Coupling and pseudo-time changed together, so their individual effects were
not isolated. Later film runs froze bulk flow, turbulence, volume fraction and relative motion.
Fluent’s steady EWF procedure assumes a converged frozen carrier; here the freeze allowed
development under fixed forcing before bulk convergence was established. (Ansys, Inc., 2025i;
2025j; 2025g)
The implicit beta algorithm lacked inner residual reports. Equal-time fields and mass accounts
supplied local checks; missing residuals were not passes. Save/reopen checked settings (Ap-
pendix A.9).

The cycle was question → bounded comparison → implementation check → Fluent solve → in-

5. Discussion
5.1. Mesh development changed the wall-liquid representation
The polyhedral–hexahedral workflow made wall refinement and coarse local testing more practi-
cal. The wall layers were intended to resolve concentrated liquid and steep cross-wall gradients.
Reduced numerical smearing remains a hypothesis because transport error also depends on res-
olution, alignment and discretisation. (Ansys, Inc., 2025c, §7.1.3.3)
Finer absorber-free cases retained liquid near the wall and base, exposing the missing drain.
Their later failure did not establish greater mesh accuracy.
Historical comparisons also changed inlet representation and preparation, and lacked matched
inventory histories. Phase 9 will test whether the liquid fields and rates persist across mesh
resolutions under one common model (Section 4.13).
5.2. Inlet phase placement changes the wall-liquid starting condition
The thin liquid strip made the wall-rich feed assumption reproducible. Films in upstream piping
provide a physical reason to test that placement, but do not establish the actual inlet profile.
(Pointon et al., 2009)
With feed, mesh and controls fixed, wall-side placement increased retained liquid by 37.4%. It
changed the liquid reaching the wall before EWF was added. Almost all supplied liquid still
left through the steam outlet, so greater retention did not establish better collection. Later wall
comparisons must preserve inlet placement.
5.3. The central development problem was the liquid collection route
The main problem was where separated liquid could leave. The closed-bottom model’s only
exit was the steam outlet. Its liquid either accumulated or left with steam, whereas separator
operation requires outward transport followed by brine discharge. Rising inventory was consis-
tent with the missing drain, but changes over steady iterations did not give a physical filling rate.
(Zarrouk & Purnanto, 2015)
The absorber-free Coupled cases discharged 99.4–99.7% of liquid feed through the steam outlet.
Their smaller mass imbalance accompanied an unsuitable liquid exit route. Coupled remained
useful after a collector was added.
Opening the lower boundary let steam leave below. Full-geometry trials exposed dependence on
a liquid seal and outlet control; neither an initially patched pool nor the tested pressure feedback
established a maintained pool. Explicit collection in the truncated vessel allowed wall studies
to continue. It represents transfer to an omitted pool without predicting its pressure, level or
discharge response.
5.4. Contact removal and staged startup made the model easier to develop
The contact absorber tied removal of mass and momentum to liquid present in the collector. This
better matched transfer to the omitted pool than prescribing removal from inlet throughput alone.
Settings readback and independent reports confirmed the intended source law.
Reduced feed allowed flow and collection to develop before full loading. The useful hold arose
after a ramp command failed; the checked ramp turned that observation into a repeatable pro-
cedure. Activating the later contact and film treatments before loading reduced ramp peaks in
continuity residual, liquid inventory and outlet flow by about 70%, 70% and 76%.



                                                 23
The shorter procedure supported finer-mesh repeats, but changed several settings together. The
cause of improvement, activation spike and later film-solver failures remained unresolved. Com-
parable starts require checked fields and film ages.
5.5. Wall treatment strongly controls the calculated liquid state
Roughness and film treatment strongly affected the liquid after it reached the wall. Small rough-
ness heights raised outlet flow, while the largest tested height reduced it by 44.09%. The selected
film package reduced mean bulk-liquid outlet flow from 24.369 to 1.735 kg/s. Both treatments
also changed inventory and spatial distribution.
A lower bulk-liquid outlet rate can accompany storage, drainage or droplet release. Large in-
ventory changes and incomplete balances prevent an efficiency interpretation. The 8 mm screen
and later 0.045 mm material-reference continuation also used different bulk advancement and
film stepping. Neither identified an optimum surface finish.
5.6. Film development, release and drainage answer different parts of the same question
Longer histories showed the film spreading while drainage remained below input. The continu-
ing growth at 500 ms answered the earlier question about patchy initial films: greater coverage
did not establish steady collection.
Later stripping and edge-separation records showed film liquid returning to droplets. Splashing
was enabled but not measured separately. This reflects the film-entrainment problem in the
literature: wall capture does not guarantee permanent collection. (Rizaldy et al., 2016; Ansys,
Inc., 2025b)
The direct drain reduced lower-wall film inventory by 70.32% with bulk equations frozen. In
the longer continuation, lower inventory stayed near 0.056 kg while upper film accumulated.
Collection therefore depended on transport to the drain as well as local removal.
The early bulk fields were fixed, and the later drain continuation failed its numerical guard.
Neither established a stationary, fully coupled solution. Persistent upper-wall storage gives a
specific reason to test features that intercept or redirect that liquid.
5.7. Contribution and the evidence needed for performance prediction
The contribution is a model-development approach that follows liquid through inlet placement,
wall transport and collection. Checked collection and startup procedures enabled the compar-
isons; longer histories distinguished storage, release and drainage. Appendix B summarises the
supported answers in Table B2.
Bulk-liquid flux, outlet quality and DPM escape fractions measure different quantities. Exact
absorber tracking did not close the early balance, and a small film-ledger error did not establish
whole-separator conservation. Each transfer must be followed and counted once.
High saved wall 𝑦+ values, incomplete refinement assessment and omitted pool response limit
performance prediction. Physical validation still requires matched operating conditions, mea-
surement locations and uncertainty. (NASA, 2021b)

6. Conclusions and future work
6.1. Conclusions
This study developed a geothermal separator CFD model by following where liquid enters, ac-
cumulates and leaves. Mesh and inlet investigations showed that the predicted wall-liquid dis-
tribution depended on its representation. In the matched inlet comparison, placing liquid beside
the wall increased retained mass by 37.4%. Absorber-free cases exposed the missing collection
route through accumulation or almost complete liquid discharge through the steam outlet.
A liquid-selective absorber and staged loading supplied common fields for wall studies. The
contact treatment represented transfer of liquid mass and momentum to the omitted brine pool.
The later combined startup reduced the peak continuity residual during the ramp by 70.15%,
while retaining a separate activation disturbance.
Wall treatment strongly changed the predicted liquid state. The selected accretion-enabled film
package reduced mean bulk-liquid outlet flow from 24.369 to 1.735 kg/s, alongside a large
inventory change. Film calculations showed spreading, thickening and drainage, with storage
still averaging 11.53 kg/s over the final 10 ms at 500 ms. Later release and direct-drain tests
showed that lower-wall collection can operate while film continues to accumulate above it.
The completed work establishes model sensitivity and checked collection and startup procedures.
It also gives a specific reason to test designs that redirect wall liquid towards collection. The
reserved Phase 9 results will assess mesh sensitivity of the combined model. Sustained liquid ac-
counting and physical validation are still needed before claiming improved separation efficiency
or pressure-drop accuracy.
6.2. Future work
The next comparisons should test the explanations established here:
   1. Resolve remaining mesh sensitivity after Phase 9. Follow up quantities that remain
      resolution-dependent, using fixed geometry and model settings at matched film ages and
      bulk-development stages.
   2. Complete the coupled liquid account. Advance the bulk equations and track bulk, film
      and droplet transfers together. Separate external removal from internal transfers and repeat
      developed-film step checks.
   3. Separate startup and model effects. Compare loading sequence, collector law and
      solver controls individually from common fields.
   4. Extend the design tests. Develop the 154k vertical-blocker film, then test the 213k angled
      ramp against controlled baselines. Compare collection, outlet liquid, storage and pressure
      loss. The ramp currently has a mesh audit; the external channel remains a supervisory
      proposal (Appendix A.14).
   5. Compare with measurements. Match feed conditions and pressure and liquid-sampling
      locations to assess the model’s use for design.
