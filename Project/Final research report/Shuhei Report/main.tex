\ESBfrontsection{Abstract}
Liquid reaching a separator wall is only one stage of separation: it must also leave through a collection route without returning to the steam. This study developed an inherited computational fluid dynamics model of a vertical geothermal steam--water separator by following liquid delivery, storage, release and removal. The simplified domain omitted brine discharge. Mesh and inlet comparisons, a numerical collector and staged startup enabled studies of roughness and Eulerian Wall Film.

Mesh development changed the predicted wall-liquid fields. In a matched comparison with mesh, feed and numerical controls held fixed, wall-side inlet placement increased retained mass by 37.4\%. Without a collector, liquid accumulated in the lower vessel or almost entirely left through the steam outlet. A liquid-selective collector represented transfer to the omitted pool, and revised startup reduced loading disturbances. Wall treatment strongly changed outlet liquid flow and inventory, but incomplete liquid accounts prevented an efficiency interpretation.

With bulk fields held fixed, film spread and thickened over the lower and middle wall. At 500 ms, input and drainage averaged 81.12 and 69.60 kg/s over the final 10 ms, while storage remained 11.53 kg/s. Later configurations added droplet release and impact mechanisms. A matched direct-drain test reduced lower-wall film by 70.32\%. However, its 0.5088 kg removal partly replaced native film outflow, giving only 0.1897 kg of additional external removal. A longer continuation showed that upper-wall film could still accumulate while lower drainage operated.

At a common film age of 195 ms, finer-mesh states showed less extensive appreciable film thickness and lower film drainage. The coarse-informed observation time did not establish equivalent film development or mesh independence.

The study establishes model sensitivity and checked collection and startup procedures. Its central finding is that outlet liquid flow and wall capture must be assessed with storage, release and external removal. Developing fields, incomplete mass balances and unresolved numerical sensitivity limit the findings to model development. Physical validation is still needed before predicting separation efficiency or pressure loss.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsection{Engineering problem}\label{engineering-problem}

Geothermal steam--water separators supply dry steam to turbines. Liquid carryover can cause erosion and transport minerals that form deposits. Separator design must therefore consider separation efficiency, pressure drop and operating pressure (\href{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geothermics.2014.05.009}{Zarrouk \& Purnanto, 2015}).

This study develops an inherited computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model of a vertical bottom-outlet cyclone with a spiral inlet, tracing the liquid path from phase placement at the inlet through wall transport and collection. It examines how the cyclone's geometry and flow behavior influence liquid separation and discharge.

\subsection{Problem in the inherited model}\label{problem-in-the-inherited-model}

In a working separator, the spiral inlet sets the incoming steam--water mixture into rotation. Liquid moves towards the vessel wall, where it can form a film and drain towards the brine pool below. The collected brine leaves through a discharge pipe, while steam leaves through a separate internal pipe (Figure 2). Some wall liquid can also return to the steam as droplets. Predicting separation therefore requires an account of what happens to liquid after it reaches the wall, as well as how it leaves the vessel.

The inherited CFD geometry omits the brine pool and discharge pipe, leaving a closed lower wall. Liquid can reach the base, but cannot drain out through it. With liquid continuously entering and no collection treatment, liquid must accumulate within the model or leave through the steam outlet. Simply opening the lower boundary does not ensure separate liquid discharge, because steam can also escape through it. The simplified geometry therefore lacks a route that removes collected liquid while retaining the intended steam path.

A second limitation concerns liquid at the vessel wall. The inherited formulation represents steam and liquid through a bulk Mixture field, without a separate model of an evolving wall film. Purnanto et al.~(2013) also tracked injected droplets using the Discrete Phase Model (DPM) to estimate outlet steam quality. These trajectories describe droplet motion through the calculated flow, but do not establish how deposited liquid forms a film, travels along the wall, accumulates or is released back into the steam. Ordinary wall boundary conditions affect the bulk flow, but do not by themselves resolve these film processes.

Together, these limitations leave the liquid path incomplete between wall capture and final discharge. A low liquid flow through the steam outlet may reflect storage on the wall or at the base rather than sustained collection. Droplet trajectories alone also cannot show whether liquid reaching a wall remains there, drains away or later returns to the steam. The model must distinguish liquid transport, storage, release and removal before its outlet predictions can support an assessment of separator performance.

\subsection{Research aim and questions}\label{research-aim-and-questions}

The aim is to develop the inherited separator CFD model and assess whether it gives a numerically consistent and physically credible account of liquid transport and collection. The investigation addresses four questions:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  How do mesh representation and inlet phase placement affect the predicted liquid distribution, retention near the wall and drainage?
\item
  Can the inherited model be developed into a credible CFD model of the separator?
\item
  How do wall roughness and wall-film modelling affect liquid transport, storage, release and collection?
\item
  How can CFD inform separator design optimisation to improve separation performance?
\end{enumerate}

Model credibility requires both numerical consistency and agreement with physical behaviour. Continuity residuals and liquid mass balances assess numerical consistency, while liquid inventories, discharge paths and predicted liquid-removal efficiency must also be physically reasonable. Efficiency comparisons must use the same definition of liquid removal and comparable operating conditions; outlet steam quality alone is a different measure. Agreement with measured separator performance provides a stronger test of physical credibility than a plausible efficiency value alone. The present work assesses progress against these criteria, while incomplete liquid accounting and the absence of a matched field comparison limit the conclusions.

\subsection{Scope and contribution}\label{scope-and-contribution}

To address these questions, the study follows the liquid path through the simplified separator model. Mesh and inlet comparisons assess how liquid reaches and remains near the wall. Collector development addresses the missing discharge route, supported by startup procedures that allow the model to reach the intended feed conditions. Wall-roughness and wall-film studies then examine how captured liquid is stored, transported, released or drained. This understanding informs two inlet-region design proposals: a vertical film blocker and an angled film ramp. Preliminary velocity-vector observations provide a partial answer to the design question by showing local flow changes consistent with the intended mechanisms; improved film drainage or separation efficiency has not been established. Downstream equipment, mineral chemistry and optimum operating pressure are outside the simulated scope.

Figure 1 brings these investigations together by distinguishing liquid transfer within the vessel from removal out of the model. Liquid entering a wall film can remain in storage, drain away or return to the steam as droplets. The routes shown were examined in separate configurations; they do not represent one fully coupled, validated model.

\section{Literature review}\label{literature-review}

\subsection{How a geothermal steam--water separator works}\label{how-a-geothermal-steamwater-separator-works}

A vertical bottom-outlet cyclone (BOC) uses a tangential or spiral inlet to rotate the incoming steam and brine. Liquid moves towards the wall while steam occupies the central region. An internal pipe carries steam out through the bottom; brine leaves separately. \emph{Bottom outlet} refers to the steam-pipe arrangement (\href{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geothermics.2014.05.009}{Zarrouk \& Purnanto, 2015}).

Liquid at the wall can form a film that drains towards the brine discharge or releases droplets back into the steam (Figure 2). The schematic also distinguishes brine discharge from downstream liquid collection and condensation.

\subsection{Internal flow physics and performance measures}\label{internal-flow-physics-and-performance-measures}

Rotation produces a low-pressure core, with inward acceleration scaling as \(u_\theta^2/r\), where \(u_\theta\) is the tangential velocity and \(r\) is the radius. The different responses of liquid and steam to pressure and drag, which depend on their densities, allow outward liquid transport. Fine droplets can follow the steam, whereas larger droplets reach collection surfaces more readily. Uncertain inlet droplet sizes and continued droplet generation complicate prediction (\href{https://publications.mygeoenergynow.org/grc/1028587.pdf}{Pointon et al., 2009}).

Higher velocity can strengthen outward transport but also increase entrainment by steam shear. Empirical data show sharp increases in outlet wetness at different inlet steam velocities for different separators (Figure 3). Thus, greater swirl alone does not establish better separation (\href{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geothermics.2014.05.009}{Zarrouk \& Purnanto, 2015}).

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\linewidth,height=0.43\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/literature/zarrouk-purnanto-2015-figure-12-breakdown.png}}
\caption[Inlet velocity and outlet wetness]{Outlet wetness and steam quality versus inlet steam velocity for three separator datasets. Left axis: outlet wetness; right axis: steam quality. Source: \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geothermics.2014.05.009}{Zarrouk and Purnanto (2015)}, fig.~12, using Bangma (1960) and Lazalde-Crabtree (1984) data.}
\label{fig:3}
\end{figure}

Outlet steam quality is defined as

\[
x_{\mathrm{out}}=
\frac{\dot m_{v,\mathrm{out}}}
{\dot m_{v,\mathrm{out}}+\dot m_{l,\mathrm{out}}},
\]

where the terms are outward vapour and liquid mass-flow rates through the steam outlet. Purnanto et al.~(2013) used this measure (\href{https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269519626_CFD_MODELLING_OF_TWO-PHASE_FLOW_INSIDE_GEOTHERMAL_STEAM-WATER_SEPARATORS}{Purnanto et al., 2013}). Outlet steam quality differs from the liquid-capture fraction, which compares collected liquid with inlet liquid; the denominators of these two measures are not interchangeable.

Steam purity concerns chemical contamination in the steam. Estimates of chemical carryover are affected by condensation and drain-pot collection, so field comparisons require matching definitions and sampling locations (\href{https://www.researchgate.net/publication/310818568_LIQUID_CARRYOVER_IN_GEOTHERMAL_STEAM-WATER_SEPARATORS}{Rizaldy et al., 2016}).

Assessing internal pressure drop requires defined inlet and outlet locations and a stated static or total pressure measure. This assessment differs from selecting the optimum operating pressure, which also involves flashing and plant conditions (\href{https://publications.mygeoenergynow.org/grc/1028587.pdf}{Pointon et al., 2009}; \href{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geothermics.2014.05.009}{Zarrouk \& Purnanto, 2015}). The present no-flashing model addresses internal flow at prescribed phase conditions.

\subsection{Established design methods and geothermal CFD}\label{established-design-methods-and-geothermal-cfd}

Empirical geothermal design methods provide reference points for CFD. However, CFD's additional flow detail must be assessed against the model's assumptions and the comparison evidence (\href{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geothermics.2014.05.009}{Zarrouk \& Purnanto, 2015}).

Pointon et al.~(2009) used Fluent's Discrete Phase Model (DPM) to compare inlet designs with assumptions about droplet feed and wall/baffle collection. Predicted efficiencies were 99.96\% for scrolled entry and 99.93\% for tangential entry; an empirical design method gave 99.955\%. Scrolled entry also gave a higher calculated mean tangential velocity than tangential entry: 34.1 rather than 32.5 m/s (Figure 4). However, agreement with the empirical method did not independently test the feed or collection assumptions (\href{https://publications.mygeoenergynow.org/grc/1028587.pdf}{Pointon et al., 2009}).

Pointon et al.~(2009) and Purnanto et al.~(2013) both used renormalisation-group (RNG) k--ε turbulence modelling. Pointon preferred RNG with a swirl modification because it had lower computational requirements than Reynolds stress modelling. This rationale offers a starting point, not a detailed turbulence-model comparison. Turbulence-model accuracy in the present swirling and near-wall flow remains to be assessed (\href{https://publications.mygeoenergynow.org/grc/1028587.pdf}{Pointon et al., 2009}; \href{https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269519626_CFD_MODELLING_OF_TWO-PHASE_FLOW_INSIDE_GEOTHERMAL_STEAM-WATER_SEPARATORS}{Purnanto et al., 2013}).

Appendix B.2 compares the methods and assumptions carried into this study.

\subsubsection{Purnanto's modelling approach and its limits}\label{purnantos-modelling-approach-and-its-limits}

Purnanto et al.~compared three separator designs using steady, isothermal Mixture modelling, RNG \(k\)--\(\varepsilon\) turbulence and SIMPLE coupling. Walls were smooth; upstream pre-separation and brine discharge were excluded, with constant pool level assumed (\href{https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269519626_CFD_MODELLING_OF_TWO-PHASE_FLOW_INSIDE_GEOTHERMAL_STEAM-WATER_SEPARATORS}{Purnanto et al., 2013, §§2.2--3.3, pp.~3--6}).

After the flow solution, DPM tracked nine Harwell-derived droplet sizes, uniformly distributed across the inlet. Equal injection counts represented unequal mass flows. Bottom-reaching droplets counted as separated; outlet quality was steam mass divided by total outlet mass. The paper's earlier escaped/injected count definition instead describes penetration (\href{https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269519626_CFD_MODELLING_OF_TWO-PHASE_FLOW_INSIDE_GEOTHERMAL_STEAM-WATER_SEPARATORS}{Purnanto et al., 2013, §§2.5 and 4.3, pp.~4, 8}). The count-based quantity can be written as:

\[
P_N=\frac{N_{\mathrm{escaped}}}{N_{\mathrm{injected}}}.
\]

Here, \(N_{\mathrm{escaped}}\) and \(N_{\mathrm{injected}}\) are particle counts. This ratio differs from outlet steam quality, defined in Section 2.2, and from the fraction of inlet liquid collected. Droplet mass weighting is therefore essential when interpreting the reported performance.

Incomplete tracks persisted even when the step limit rose from \(10^5\) to \(10^6\). Counting them as separated improved empirical agreement, but the authors acknowledged that assumption was not rigorous. Exact injection weights were unreported, and mesh refinement and experimental calibration remained necessary (\href{https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269519626_CFD_MODELLING_OF_TWO-PHASE_FLOW_INSIDE_GEOTHERMAL_STEAM-WATER_SEPARATORS}{Purnanto et al., 2013, pp.~8--9}).

Differences in vessel proportions also prevented an isolated comparison of inlet shape. For the present study, a further limitation is the distinction between assigning droplets a destination and resolving liquid transport after wall capture. The approach did not provide a separate evolving wall-film account through which storage, drainage and return to the steam could be assessed. These limitations motivate explicit collection and wall-film treatments, while the Mixture and turbulence formulations provide a starting point for model development.

\subsection{Wall--liquid transport and carryover}\label{wallliquid-transport-and-carryover}

Film motion depends on gravity, pressure and shear. Deposition adds liquid to the film; drainage removes liquid from the film; entrainment returns droplets to the steam. More wall deposition can therefore increase liquid storage or later release without increasing external collection.

Rizaldy et al.~(2016) predict that entrainment is sensitive to loading, film thickness and inlet velocity. Figure 5 illustrates the conceptual mechanisms of undercut and roll-wave entrainment, associated with lower and higher film Reynolds numbers, respectively. Film Reynolds number compares inertia with viscosity using liquid velocity and film thickness. However, the brine-flow data needed to compare the predicted liquid carryover with measurements from the first drain pot after the Wairakei separator were unavailable, leaving quantitative agreement with field measurements unresolved (\href{https://www.researchgate.net/publication/310818568_LIQUID_CARRYOVER_IN_GEOTHERMAL_STEAM-WATER_SEPARATORS}{Rizaldy et al., 2016}).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\linewidth,height=0.43\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/literature/rizaldy-2016-figure-9-entrainment.png}}
\caption[Droplet entrainment from a wall film]{Conceptual mechanisms by which steam shear releases droplets from a liquid film: (a) undercut and (b) roll-wave entrainment. Source: \href{https://www.researchgate.net/publication/310818568_LIQUID_CARRYOVER_IN_GEOTHERMAL_STEAM-WATER_SEPARATORS}{Rizaldy et al.~(2016)}, fig.~9, crediting Ishii and Grolmes (1975).}
\label{fig:5}
\end{figure}

Fluent's roughness settings change near-wall momentum transfer; they do not add film storage or a drain (\href{https://ansyshelp.ansys.com/public/Views/Secured/corp/v252/en/flu_th/x1-5720008.164.html}{Ansys, Inc., 2025k}). EWF, by contrast, adds surface storage and transport. The effects of roughness and EWF on outlet flow must be interpreted alongside retained liquid mass and collection.

\subsection{Representing liquid and its collection}\label{representing-liquid-and-its-collection}

The Mixture model represents bulk liquid through the secondary-phase volume fraction. Algebraic slip estimates the relative velocity between phases under a local-equilibrium assumption. The liquid volume fraction does not directly resolve film thickness or the droplet-size distribution (\href{https://ansyshelp.ansys.com/public/Views/Secured/corp/v252/en/flu_th/flu_th_sec_drift_oview.html}{Ansys, Inc., 2025d}).

DPM follows size-dependent droplet trajectories. Interpreting its results requires defined injection and boundary treatments and a count of every tracked fate, including incomplete trajectories.

Skoog (2020) combined EWF surface transport, DPM deposition and entrainment in a reactor-channel approximation. Film and droplet flows were compared with one-dimensional calculations using Okawa correlations based on experiments. The inlet liquid allocation between film and droplets and the transverse droplet velocity affected the result. This separate accounting of film and droplet flows provides a precedent, but the applicability of the reactor conditions and correlations to a cyclone still requires justification (\href{https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1452100/FULLTEXT02.pdf}{Skoog, 2020}; \href{https://ansyshelp.ansys.com/public/views/secured/corp/v252/en/flu_ug/flu_ug_models_wallfilm.html}{Ansys, Inc., 2025e}).

Bulk-to-film transfer moves liquid within the domain, whereas the absorber removes it from the domain. Droplet injection must not duplicate liquid already supplied by another feed. Fluent permits cell-zone mass sources and associated transport sources (\href{https://ansyshelp.ansys.com/public/Views/Secured/corp/v252/en/flu_ug/flu_ug_bcs_sec_cell_zones.html}{Ansys, Inc., 2025a}), but the governing source law and its effects require assessment. Section 3 describes the contact-based absorber.

\subsection{Numerical startup, convergence and validation}\label{numerical-startup-convergence-and-validation}

In a steady calculation, iterations and pseudo-time describe numerical development, not physical filling time. The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) recommends monitoring residuals and relevant outputs, with convergence criteria based on acceptable error in those outputs (\href{https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/wind/valid/tutorial/iterconv.html}{National Aeronautics and Space Administration {[}NASA{]}, 2021a}).

Fluent permits flow development before film initialisation (\href{https://ansyshelp.ansys.com/public/views/secured/corp/v252/en/flu_ug/flu_ug_ewf_sec_overview.html}{Ansys, Inc., 2025h}). Reduced-feed startup and ramps remain case-specific choices that require assessment.

Implementation checks, iterative convergence, mass accounting and numerical sensitivity answer separate questions. Mesh and time-step studies require a fixed model; changes to roughness or the absorber instead test model sensitivity (\href{https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/wind/valid/tutorial/verassess.html}{NASA, 2021c}).

Physical validation requires comparison with relevant measurements and assessment of uncertainty (\href{https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/wind/valid/tutorial/valassess.html}{NASA, 2021b}). For this model, those comparisons must assess the assumed collection route and outlet predictions within the tested operating range.

\subsection{Remaining model uncertainties and research direction}\label{remaining-model-uncertainties-and-research-direction}

The literature shows that the definition of collection is central to the research problem. In Pointon's model, a droplet's trajectory ends at a collecting boundary, whereas the film literature allows captured liquid to return to the steam. Purnanto's incomplete tracks highlight a further distinction between a calculated destination and an assumed one. Agreement in an outlet measure therefore does not, by itself, resolve the internal liquid path when feed, capture or final fate is imposed.

The inherited model adds a specific difficulty: its truncated domain omits brine discharge. Changes to the mesh and inlet can alter predicted liquid delivery to the wall, but cannot provide the missing exit. This study therefore connects comparisons of these changes to collector development and then to film storage, release and drainage. The separate bulk, film and droplet accounts make it possible to ask whether a lower bulk-liquid outlet rate accompanies external collection, internal transfer or continued storage. That distinction determines what each result contributes to performance prediction.

\subsection{Providing a liquid-collection route}\label{providing-a-liquid-collection-route}

The inlet and mesh studies changed delivery and retention but provided no separate collection route. Earlier trials included the lower geometry to represent the brine pool and discharge directly. Unresolved stability and mass-balance problems in those trials complicated the study of liquid transport (Appendix A.7). The investigation therefore returned to the simplified domain to examine transport and collection separately from pool behaviour and discharge control. Full-geometry modelling remained a possible later extension.

The absorber and closed bottom wall together represented the two functions of the omitted brine pool needed for this study. The absorber captured liquid reaching the lower region, representing its transfer into the pool. For the steam, the bottom wall acted as an idealised stationary pool surface: it prevented downward passage and diverted the flow within the vessel towards the steam outlet. This was the basis for selecting the simplified model for liquid-transport and collection studies: it retained liquid capture and steam diversion without resolving pool motion, level control or discharge. The rigid, no-slip surface remained an approximation to the real steam--brine interface.

The absorber was implemented as a liquid mass sink in a defined cell region near the base. Removal depended on the local liquid content and a prescribed depletion time:

\[
S_L=-\frac{\rho_L\alpha_L}{\tau},\qquad \tau=10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s}.
\]

Here, \(\rho_L\) is liquid density, \(\alpha_L\) is liquid volume fraction and \(\tau\) is the depletion time; \(S_L\) is the mass sink per unit volume, in kg/(m³·s). Associated momentum and turbulence sources represented capture without a direct steam mass sink. This allowed liquid reaching the lower region to leave without explicitly modelling the pool and discharge pipe. Appendix A.3 gives the source laws, accounting checks and the record of depletion-time tests (\href{https://ansyshelp.ansys.com/public/Views/Secured/corp/v252/en/flu_ug/flu_ug_bcs_sec_cell_zones.html}{Ansys, Inc., 2025a}).

Several settings changed together, so this comparison tested the combined procedure rather than isolating each contribution (Appendix A.1).

The shortened procedure reused the reduced-feed field without reinitialisation and activated contact collection, roughness, Coupled flow and a dry-initialised film before increasing the feed (Appendix A.1).

Early activation reduced disturbances during the loading ramp, but introduced a larger continuity-residual peak during the preceding activation hold. Within the ramp window, the peak continuity residual fell by 70.15\%, peak bulk + film inventory by 70.43\%, and peak outward outlet liquid flow by 75.78\% relative to the historical ramp (Figure 14). The \href{draft/../../../experiments/phase-07-2a-wall-liquid-routing/stage-03-shortened-reconstruction/early-ewf-startup/results.md}{startup analysis} retains the peak values behind these reductions.

The activation hold, excluded from Figure 14, had a continuity residual peak of 0.10593, exceeding the historical ramp peak. At iteration 5080, bulk and film masses were 61.0549 and 0.1645 kg, respectively. Almost all film input was still stored (Appendix A.3). All ramp updates met the recorded inner-film tolerance, but 15 of the final 100 hold updates failed to meet it (Appendix A.4). The benefit was therefore specific to the loading ramp; the activation disturbance and later inner-film tolerance failures remained.

The shortened startup established a more practical route to the combined full-feed model, reducing the ramp peaks in continuity residual, liquid inventory and outlet liquid flow by about 70--76\%. The contact source matched independently evaluated removal throughout the procedure, confirming its implementation. Despite the remaining activation disturbance and balance limitations, this provided a defined starting field for longer film studies. The central question could then shift from reaching full feed to whether the young film would develop sustained drainage or continue to store liquid.

\subsection{Film spreading, drainage and continued storage}\label{film-spreading-drainage-and-continued-storage}

The young film produced during startup was advanced to 500 ms to examine whether it would spread and develop sustained drainage. The surrounding bulk flow was held fixed while the film evolved, allowing its response to a constant supply and forcing to be examined. This isolated film development; the retained bulk field was still developing, so the calculation did not represent a converged whole-separator flow. Native film-edge outflow provided drainage, while the bulk absorber acted separately on liquid outside the film (Appendix A.5).

Drainage rose rapidly during early development and then began to level out (Figure 15). This showed that the film could sustain an outflow rather than only store incoming liquid. However, the drainage curve remained below the film-input curve, and the film inventory continued to rise through 500 ms. Over the final reporting window, drainage was still approximately 14\% below input. A slowly changing drainage rate therefore did not indicate a steady film.

The spatial views show the accompanying spread and thickening across much of the vessel wall, particularly in the lower and middle regions (Figure 16). The initially thin film developed into broader regions of appreciable thickness, showing that the model could represent sustained surface transport and storage. These snapshots extend to 265 ms; the later development to 500 ms is recorded by the histories in Figure 15. All spatial views use the same thickness scale.

Computational cost was a major practical limitation. Small film timesteps required many updates to advance even a short physical time. Timestep size, Courant limits and mesh resolution had to be considered together when seeking faster progress without losing numerical stability or acceptable film-mass accounting. A larger step that passed an early check did not necessarily remain suitable as the film developed; later checks rejected some faster settings. Finding workable settings was therefore an iterative task, and extending the calculation far enough to test whether inventory would eventually level out remained costly. Appendix A.4 retains the timestep checks and solver limitations.

The calculation established widespread film development and sustained drainage, but not a stationary film inventory. Continued storage remained the central limitation at 500 ms; it was unresolved whether a longer run would reach a balance. The next studies examined whether additional release and removal routes could change that balance and reduce continued storage.

\subsection{Film release and direct lower-wall drainage}\label{film-release-and-direct-lower-wall-drainage}

Continued film storage raised two questions: could liquid return to droplets, and could it be removed directly at the base? A separate configuration enabled additional film forces and droplet interactions, including stripping and edge separation. These release mechanisms were active, but film inventory still increased. Release returned liquid to droplets within the model; it did not itself represent external collection. Because the bulk fields and film settings also changed, this was not an isolated comparison with the 500 ms run (Appendix A.5).

The next test addressed direct collection. The bulk absorber could not remove liquid stored in the wall film, so the film domain was extended onto the lower wall and a separate sink removed local film mass and its associated momentum. A matched Drain OFF/ON comparison used identical starting fields, the same expanded film domain and frozen bulk flow. Over 15 ms, activating the sink reduced lower-wall film inventory by about 70\% (Figure 17). Total external removal also increased, although part of the direct removal replaced native film outflow.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth,height=0.43\textheight,keepaspectratio]{\detokenize{figures/results-direct-film-drain.png}}
\caption[Direct drainage and external removal]{Drain OFF/ON comparison: (a) lower-wall film mass and (b) external liquid removal.}

During longer development, lower-wall film inventory stayed low while upper-film inventory continued to grow. This accumulation was already present before the run exceeded its Courant limit; the rejected tail does not establish a reliable endpoint (Appendix A.5).

Direct drainage therefore controlled storage locally, but did not stop accumulation elsewhere. Adding a removal route was insufficient to establish a steady film: liquid supply and transport towards that route also needed to be understood. This motivated the later mesh comparison, which examined how resolution affected film distribution, storage and drainage.

\subsection{Numerical assessment and mesh sensitivity}\label{numerical-assessment-and-mesh-sensitivity}

The coarse mesh provided a practical basis for developing film transport and collection. The next question was whether its predicted film coverage, storage and drainage persisted with finer resolution. Four meshes---60k, 342k, 680k and 997k---were compared at a common film age of 195 ms. Earlier coarse-mesh drainage had begun to level out around 200 ms (Figure 15), making this a practical observation time rather than an equilibrium criterion. The earlier film settings differed, so its development timescale was only a guide. Computational cost and available time were the main reasons for using the shortened startup and limiting the mesh comparison to about 200 ms of film development.

Each film evolved under its own mesh's frozen bulk field, using the selected steel roughness and with film-to-flow momentum feedback disabled. The comparison therefore tested developing states under the selected preparation procedure, rather than isolating spatial discretisation error. Appendix A.6 gives the preparation differences and detailed values.

The clearest difference appears in the wall-film contours (Figure 18). These maps unfold the cylindrical wall, with height vertically and angle around the vessel horizontally; the white opening marks the inlet. The lower row compares all meshes at 195 ms. On the 60k mesh, relatively thick film covers much of the displayed wall. The finer meshes retain more distinct film paths and thinner upper-wall regions. The 342k and 680k patterns are similar, while the 997k case has less extensive appreciable film thickness above the inlet. Purple indicates low thickness, not necessarily a dry wall. All panels share a 0--0.30 mm scale; thicker values are clipped, and the arrows indicate direction rather than speed.

The drain rate depends on the liquid present in the lower-wall removal region. Under the same removal law, less local film therefore gives a lower drain rate; this does not independently show that the drain became less effective. Figure 19 shows instantaneous rates at saved states, not cumulative collection. Its bulk quantities describe the frozen forcing, while the 2.6M case provides only an initial reference and has no 195 ms film result.

The contours are consistent with slower film development on the finer meshes, but they cannot establish propagation speed. Lower supply or greater release could also produce thinner, less extensive films. Differences in retained bulk fields and preparation further limit causal attribution. Source and timestep checks supported implementation, but continuous selected-run histories were unavailable to separate film supply, storage, release and removal (Appendices A.4 and A.6).

The mesh comparison changed the interpretation of the coarse-mesh results: widespread film coverage and strong drainage were not reproduced at the same film age on the finer meshes. The study established sensitivity to mesh and preparation, but not mesh independence. Longer histories are needed to determine whether the finer cases develop towards similar behaviour or retain different distributions. \subsection{Experiment workflow and use of automation}\label{experiment-workflow-and-use-of-automation}

Automation made repeated CFD investigations practical by connecting case setup, Fluent execution and analysis within a reusable workflow. OpenAI Codex helped perform these tasks, while I retained responsibility for the research questions and interpretation. The main contribution was continuity: later experiments could build on saved cases, checked procedures and recorded findings rather than reconstructing the work each time.

The retained records identify 387 executed configurations across both project partner and I, including failed and partial trials. This indicates the breadth of exploration, not a measured speed-up from automation. Reusing setup and analysis procedures supported comparisons across inlet conditions, collection methods, roughness and wall-film settings. Checkpoints and background monitoring also allowed long calculations to continue beyond an active chat and preserved starting points for recovery.

An experienced researcher may use the same workflow more effectively by identifying useful tests earlier. The project's experience suggests that agents can take on much of the repeated implementation and evidence-handling work, while the value of that work depends on informed scientific direction.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

This project developed the inherited separator model into a framework for investigating liquid transport and collection. Its progress should be assessed through the additional questions the model could answer: where liquid accumulated, how it entered and moved within a wall film, and whether it reached an external removal route. Lower residuals and repeatable startup procedures made these investigations practical, while the resulting fields identified where further model development was needed.

\subsection{Inlet and mesh representation}\label{inlet-and-mesh-representation}

The early comparisons showed why outlet flux alone could not explain the separator's behaviour. Pure-split feed produced a clearer liquid-rich wall band and deeper enrichment near the base, although almost all incoming liquid still left through the steam outlet (Section 4.1). The contours therefore revealed a change in liquid distribution that the boundary fluxes largely concealed. This made the path from wall capture to collection the central modelling question.

The finer absorber-free cases exposed the same issue through continued accumulation at the closed base (Section 4.2). Lower liquid flow through the steam outlet could reflect retention rather than successful separation. The mesh comparisons suggested that near-wall representation affected how much liquid remained there, but differences in inlet placement and numerical preparation prevented an isolated accuracy judgement. Together, these findings showed that changing liquid delivery and retention could not resolve the missing discharge route. That required an explicit collection treatment.

\subsection{Collection and numerical stability}\label{collection-and-numerical-stability}

Purnanto's collection assumptions provided the starting point described in Section 2.3.1, but the present study needed an explicit route for removing liquid from the bulk field. In the simplified domain, the lack of a separate liquid exit led either to accumulation or discharge with steam. The absorber supplied an explicit collection route without requiring a resolved brine pool and its outlet controls. This removed a practical obstacle to studying wall transport, while retaining pool pressure and level response as modelling assumptions.

The numerical progress was substantial. With collection and reduced feed, the minimum continuity residual was about 650 times lower than the saved absorber-free reference, while liquid inventory was about 93\% lower and changed little late in the hold (Section 4.4). These comparisons involve different loading, meshes and sampling points, so they demonstrate progress in the combined procedure rather than the effect of the absorber alone. More directly, the shortened-startup comparison reduced ramp peaks in continuity residual, liquid inventory and outlet flow by about 70--76\%. This made it practical to introduce roughness, film physics and collection before full loading and then reuse the resulting fields for further experiments.

Strong swirl, turbulence and exchanges between bulk liquid, wall film and droplets made this a coupled problem. A change that improved one response could expose another unresolved process. The reduced residuals therefore mattered because they supported further investigation, not because they removed the need to assess storage, conservation and physical behaviour.

\subsection{Wall-liquid transport and film development}\label{wall-liquid-transport-and-film-development}

The wall studies identified a more specific problem than liquid reaching the vessel wall. Roughness substantially changed outlet liquid flow, but its response was not monotonic and could accompany depletion of stored liquid. The commercial-steel reference therefore supplied a material-based setting for later work, rather than a roughness chosen simply to minimise outlet flow. Roughness affected transport without creating a new collection route.

Extending the trajectory-based treatment discussed in Section 2.3.1, EWF made the wall a separate liquid store with its own transport and release processes. The model progressed from a young film to widespread coverage and sustained drainage over 500 ms. This was a significant extension of the model's capability: film development could be inspected directly rather than inferred only from bulk contours or droplet destinations. However, film input still exceeded drainage, showing why a slowly changing drainage rate was insufficient evidence of a stationary liquid state.

The release and direct-drain tests clarified the destination of liquid leaving the film. Release returned liquid to droplets within the vessel; direct drainage removed it externally. The lower-wall sink reduced local inventory while some of its removal replaced native outflow. During longer development, lower-wall storage remained low but upper-film storage continued to increase. These findings show that a functioning collector does not guarantee that the whole film reaches a steady balance.

This directs attention towards transport between wall capture and collection. A design feature should be assessed by whether it increases externally collected liquid without increasing carryover, continued storage or unacceptable pressure loss. A lower film inventory alone could instead reflect greater release into the steam. This provided the physical basis for the design proposals in Section 5.5.

\subsection{Mesh sensitivity of the developed model}\label{mesh-sensitivity-of-the-developed-model}

The mesh comparison tested whether the behaviour used to develop the model persisted with finer resolution. At the same film age, the finer meshes showed more distinct film paths, less extensive upper-wall thickness, lower inventory and lower drainage. The apparent agreement between the intermediate meshes did not persist at the finest completed mesh (Section 4.10). This establishes sensitivity to mesh and preparation, and prevents treating coarse-mesh drainage as a mesh-independent performance prediction.

Slower film propagation is one possible explanation, but the lower inventory makes a transport-only explanation incomplete. Film accumulation depends on supply minus drainage and release. If starting mass, supply and release were identical, persistently lower drainage would leave more liquid stored, not less. The selected endpoints instead contain less film as well as a lower instantaneous drain rate. Differences in supply, release and their histories must therefore be considered alongside transport; an endpoint does not establish the preceding drainage history.

Broad coarse-mesh coverage could also partly reflect numerical spreading, while finer resolution preserves more distinct paths. Differences in the retained bulk fields and preparation could produce similar contrasts. These alternatives cannot be separated with the available snapshots. The useful result is that a comparison time informed by the coarse mesh did not establish equivalent film development across resolutions. Longer histories are needed to determine whether the finer cases approach similar behaviour later or maintain different liquid distributions.

\subsection{Implications for separator design}\label{implications-for-separator-design}

The wall-film studies showed that reaching the wall was only one stage of separation: liquid could accumulate above the collector or return to the steam. This understanding guided two proposals that act on the liquid path after capture. The \textbf{vertical film blocker} aims to interrupt circulating wall liquid before it meets the incoming steam again. The \textbf{angled film ramp} aims to guide that liquid downwards towards collection. They provide a partial answer to the design-optimisation question in Section 1.3 by translating the observed transport problem into specific geometric changes.

Figure 21 shows encouraging local flow responses: the blocker interrupts the vector pattern, while flow beside the ramp changes direction along its inclined edge. These observations support further testing of the intended mechanisms. They do not establish improved drainage, because the vectors describe mixture velocity rather than wall-film motion. Different views and vector sampling also limit the panels to a qualitative comparison.

However, their meshes and earlier preparation differed, the bulk fields were frozen, and stripping and edge separation were disabled. The next design comparison must include film release and coupled transport to test whether either feature reduces liquid returning to the steam. The separate enhanced-film inlet comparison must remain distinct from these basic-film results.

The design contribution is therefore a pair of physically motivated proposals with preliminary evidence of local flow redirection. Their effect on wall-film transport and sustained collection remains to be tested, as set out in Section 6.2.

\subsection{Overall contribution and remaining limits}\label{overall-contribution-and-remaining-limits}

The project substantially expanded the inherited model's capability and made the liquid-collection problem more explicit. Checked removal and startup procedures supported repeatable wall studies; longer film calculations distinguished storage from drainage; and mesh comparisons challenged conclusions that could otherwise have been drawn from one resolution. The resulting wall-film understanding also informed two design proposals. Relative to the inherited Purnanto approach (Section 2.3.1), the advance is a broader, testable account of liquid transport and collection. It does not establish a corrected efficiency for Purnanto's cases, which were not reproduced under matched conditions.

These procedures emerged from a broader programme than the selected report sequence. The retained records identify 387 executed configurations across both project partners, including failed and partial trials. Investigations of alternative boundaries, pool treatments, turbulence models and numerical controls informed which approaches were retained and which questions required a different method. Appendix A.7 gives the pool-control evidence behind the move to simplified collection. Their value was in narrowing the modelling problem and developing reusable procedures; the number of runs alone does not establish accuracy.

Computational cost limited how far those tests could be taken. Mesh resolution, film timestep, Courant limits and solver stability had to be balanced against available time. Shortened startup and the approximately 200 ms mesh comparison were practical responses to those constraints. Frozen bulk fields allowed film development to be studied under fixed forcing, but did not resolve the complete evolving interaction between bulk liquid, film and droplets. The finite histories therefore leave eventual stationary behaviour open.

The agent harness supported this breadth of investigation through reusable setup, persistent solver access, checkpoint recovery and traceable evidence (Section 4.11). Its contribution was practical execution and continuity across experiments. Scientific judgement was still required to select useful questions and distinguish a completed calculation from a credible prediction.

The remaining steps are specific: close the combined liquid account, distinguish mesh effects from preparation and development time, and compare with measurements under matched conditions. These limits constrain claims about separation efficiency and pressure loss, but do not negate the demonstrated progress. The project established a more capable experimental model and identified the liquid pathways on which further numerical and design work should focus.

\section{Conclusions and future work}\label{conclusions-and-future-work}

\subsection{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

Liquid reaching the wall did not by itself establish collection. This study developed a geothermal separator CFD model by tracking where liquid enters, accumulates, transfers and leaves. Mesh and inlet investigations showed that the predicted wall-liquid distribution depended on how wall liquid was represented. In the matched inlet comparison, placing liquid beside the wall increased retained mass by 37.4\%. Absorber-free cases showed the consequences of a missing collection route: liquid accumulated or was discharged almost completely through the steam outlet.

A liquid-selective absorber and staged loading supplied common fields for wall studies. The contact treatment represented the transfer of liquid mass and momentum to the omitted brine pool. The later combined startup reduced the peak continuity residual during the ramp by 70.15\%, but a separate activation disturbance remained.

Wall treatment changed both bulk-liquid discharge and liquid storage in the model. The selected accretion-enabled film package reduced mean bulk-liquid outlet flow from 24.369 to 1.735 kg/s, alongside a large decrease in bulk-liquid inventory. With the bulk fields held fixed, the film calculations predicted spreading, thickening and drainage, but the film storage rate still averaged 11.53 kg/s over the final 10 ms ending at 500 ms. The direct drain removed 0.5088 kg but increased combined external removal by only 0.1897 kg because it partly replaced native film outflow. The mass removed by the direct drain alone therefore overstated the additional collection. These calculations showed that lower-wall collection could operate while film continued to accumulate above it.

The completed work demonstrates model sensitivity and provides checked procedures for collection and startup. These findings motivate tests of designs that redirect wall liquid towards collection. At 195 ms, finer-mesh states showed less extensive appreciable film thickness and lower film drainage. The coarse-informed observation time did not establish equivalent film development across resolutions. This is consistent with slower propagation, but differences in film supply and release remain unresolved; mesh independence has not been established. Sustained liquid accounting and physical validation are still needed before claiming improved separation efficiency or pressure-drop accuracy.

The fourth research question is partially answered through the vertical film blocker and angled film ramp (Section 5.5). Understanding wall-film accumulation shifted the design focus towards interrupting circulating liquid and guiding it towards collection. Preliminary velocity vectors show local flow changes consistent with those intentions, providing a positive basis for further testing. They do not yet establish improved wall-film drainage or an optimised separator design.

\subsection{Future work}\label{future-work}

The first priority is to determine whether the wall film can reach a statistically steady state. At 500 ms, drainage had become fairly steady but film inventory was still increasing (Section 4.8). Longer calculations should track total and upper-wall inventory alongside film input, release and drainage. A steady film would require no sustained inventory growth over a sufficiently long interval, with mean input balanced by mean outflow and release. Extending the fixed-bulk calculation would first test this balance under unchanged forcing; it would not establish a steady state for the fully coupled separator. Given the computational cost, timestep and Courant settings should be assessed on a developed film before committing to longer runs.

The same investigation should resolve whether finer meshes need more development time. At approximately 200 ms, the finest completed mesh had both less film and lower drainage, so slower transport alone could not explain the difference (Section 4.10). Longer histories of film supply, storage and drainage on selected meshes would show whether their responses approach one another or remain distinct. This is more informative than adding further comparisons at the same short film age.

The second priority is to test whether the blocker and ramp proposed in Section 5.5 reduce upper-wall accumulation. Compare both features with the unmodified separator under matched feed, film settings and preparation, tracking upper-wall inventory, external collection and liquid returning to the steam. Film release and coupled transport must be included to assess renewed carryover. A useful feature must reduce persistent storage by directing liquid towards collection, rather than merely releasing it back into the steam.

\subsection{Collection law and incomplete liquid closure}\label{a.3.-collection-law-and-incomplete-liquid-closure}

The contact collector in Section 4.3 acts on 715 cells selected by centroid \(y\leq0.10\) m:

\[
S_L=-\rho_L\max(\alpha_L,0)/\tau,\qquad \tau=10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s}.
\]

It removes liquid momentum with the liquid velocity and includes associated \(k\) and \(\varepsilon\) sources, without a direct vapour mass sink. The evaluated positive removal \(M_c/\tau\) and native signed applied-source report describe the same source; a balance must count it once. The depletion time is an assumed capture parameter, not a measured residence time. Short common-parent strength tests did not establish an optimum or stationary throughput (\href{draft/../../../experiments/phase-07-2a-wall-liquid-routing/stage-02-combined-ewf-roughness/ewf-absorber/setup.md}{contact setup and implementation}; \href{draft/../../../experiments/phase-07-2a-wall-liquid-routing/stage-02-combined-ewf-roughness/results.md\#all-liquid-contact-absorber-trial}{strength tests}).

The historical startup and first wall screens used a different, throughput-controlled source:

\[
S_{L,\mathrm{throughput}}=-\frac{\dot m_{L,\mathrm{in}}\alpha_L}
{\max(\int_{V_c}\alpha_L\,\mathrm dV,10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3})}.
\]

\subsection{Evidence for simplifying the collection model}\label{a.7.-evidence-for-simplifying-the-collection-model}

Earlier full-geometry trials explain the choice in Section 4.3 to study liquid transport without resolving a pool. \subsection{Design evidence and its scope}\label{a.8.-design-evidence-and-its-scope}
